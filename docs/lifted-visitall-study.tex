\begin{abstract}
We test a small \texttt{folplan} planner on eight official five-dimensional
hard-to-ground VisitAll instances. Lifted joins replace complete Cartesian
action grounding; an admissible goal-cover bound then guides A*. Under a 1 GiB
memory cap and 15-second timeout, the older fully grounded indexed version
solves one instance, lifted BFS solves six, and lifted A* solves seven.
Powerlifted and the newer Tyr planner solve all eight. In same-host runs,
A* has lower full-process latency than Powerlifted on five of the seven
instances it solves and than Tyr on four shallow instances. On \texttt{p8},
the times are 0.0073, 0.1273, and 0.0260 seconds, respectively. The
comparators are faster on deeper cases; these latency wins identify a
narrow use case rather than general planning superiority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and design}
Classical PDDL actions are schematic. Grounding all parameter tuples can
dominate planning even when only a few actions are applicable in each state.
Corr\^ea et al.\ describe lifted successor generation as conjunctive query
evaluation~\cite{correa2020}. The indexed \texttt{folplan} baseline still
grounded every typed parameter tuple before search~\cite{priorstudy}.
Powerlifted is a strong published lifted baseline~\cite{powerlifted}. A newer
Tyr study reports greater hard-to-ground coverage using parallel semi-naive
Datalog evaluation~\cite{tyr2026}; a Powerlifted comparison alone is not a
claim of overall state-of-the-art performance.

For each schema, our new generator collects positive atoms reached only
through conjunction in its precondition. It joins their arguments against
facts in the current state, binding action parameters, then enumerates any
unbound parameters over their declared object types. The full first-order
precondition is evaluated for every candidate. Disjunction, negation,
implication, and quantified subformulas contribute no join constraint;
schemas without a usable positive atom use bounded Cartesian fallback.
Candidates are ordered by schema and original object order, preserving the
breadth-first search tie break. At most 256 positive conjuncts enter the
join; the complete formula remains checked. The ground-action limit now
counts distinct candidates emitted across search states, before the full
formula test, rather than the full Cartesian universe.

The five-dimensional VisitAll move schemas each have six \texttt{pos}
parameters. They require one five-argument \texttt{at-robot} fact and one
\texttt{neighbor} pair. In a state with one robot location, these relations
bind almost every parameter before effects are applied. With 24 objects,
naive grounding would enumerate $5\cdot24^6=955{,}514{,}880$ actions.

\section{Experimental protocol}
We use official \texttt{5-dim-visitall-CLOSE-g1} instances
\texttt{p0--p5,p8,p9} from the HTG corpus at commit
\texttt{c5670ed2}~\cite{htg}.
These eight instances contain 6--24 position objects; their different
goals require 3--13 steps, so the horizontal axis is not a pure size
scaling curve. We compare the prior indexed \texttt{folplan}, lifted
\texttt{folplan}, Fast Downward 26.6+ at commit \texttt{9b81c7e} with
\texttt{astar(blind())}, and Powerlifted at commit \texttt{bf54e169}
using \texttt{alt-bfws1}, FF, Yannakakis joins, and unit costs. The latter
is a competitive lifted heuristic planner~\cite{powerlifted}.

All binaries use release builds on one AMD Ryzen 5 7640U machine running
Linux 7.2.5. Each run gets a 1 GiB address-space cap and a 15-second
process timeout. Both \texttt{folplan} versions use a 100{,}000-state and
1{,}000{,}000-ground-action setting, with the changed candidate-limit
meaning described above. Wall time includes process startup, parsing,
translation, and search. Solved results are medians of five interleaved
runs; a solver that hit a limit was recorded once per instance. Powerlifted's
Python driver is included in its full-process time. Raw measurements and
status codes are in \path{benchmarks/results/htg-visitall-raw.csv}; summary
data are in \path{benchmarks/results/htg-visitall-summary.csv}.
The command-line harness is \path{benchmarks/run_visitall.py}.

Lifted joins remove the immediate grounding failure: \texttt{p1} has
$5\cdot8^6=1{,}310{,}720$ raw action tuples, already above the baseline's
cap. On \texttt{p8}, the lifted planner is $5.57\times$ faster than
Powerlifted in full-process time; on \texttt{p1}, $9.43\times$ faster.
This advantage is chiefly startup latency, not faster search: Powerlifted
reports roughly 0.002 seconds of internal search on \texttt{p8}, while its
full process takes 0.118 seconds. It expands only three states, versus
74 states explored by our breadth-first search.

At five or more steps, search dominates. Powerlifted is $2.68\times$
faster on \texttt{p2} and $11.75\times$ faster on \texttt{p4}; lifted
\texttt{folplan} reaches the memory cap on \texttt{p3} and \texttt{p9}.
All reported solved cases have matching plan lengths, but Powerlifted's
configuration does not in general guarantee optimality. The results
suggest that heuristic guidance and a more compact state representation,
not more grounding work, are the next bottlenecks.

\section{Admissible A* in the lifted planner}
We retain lifted successor joins and use the \texttt{Agent} graph-search
library for both BFS and A*. For a positive conjunctive goal, the new
goal-cover lower bound divides the number of missing goal atoms by an upper
bound on how many one action can add. Each VisitAll move adds exactly one
\texttt{visited} fact, so the estimate is the number of unvisited goal facts.
The full Cartesian grounding exceeds the 10,000-action threshold on these
instances; delete-relaxed $h_{\max}$ is therefore skipped. Search and its
replay check still evaluate the exact action and goal formulas. A* returns
shortest plans with unit costs; Powerlifted's BFWS+FF configuration is
satisficing and has no general optimality guarantee.

We built \texttt{folplan} in release mode at commit \texttt{5c0360c} with
\texttt{Agent} commit \texttt{7bee29a}. Powerlifted uses clean commit
\texttt{bf54e169}, plus the archived
\path{benchmarks/powerlifted-gcc16-compat.patch} adapting removed allocator
APIs for GCC 16; the patch changes no search logic. Its release build uses
\texttt{-Wno-error=stringop-overflow}. Both planners ran on the same AMD
Ryzen 5 7640U under a 1 GiB address-space limit and 15-second wall timeout.
\texttt{folplan} used 100,000 states and 1,000,000 distinct action candidates;
Powerlifted used \texttt{alt-bfws1}, FF, Yannakakis joins, and unit costs.
We alternated solver order and report medians of five full-process runs for
each solved case. A failed solver was run once per instance. No other build
ran during measurement. CPU frequency was not fixed. Raw rows, statuses,
metadata, and the harness are in
\path{benchmarks/results/astar-bfs-powerlifted-visitall/} and
\path{benchmarks/run_heuristic_visitall.py}.

We separately ran the newer Tyr executable at commit \texttt{e0ea47a} on
the same host and corpus, after the first session had finished. Its lifted
greedy best-first search used RPG-FF, unit costs, one Datalog thread, one
search worker, and default preferred actions, matching the single-core
algorithm family in its study~\cite{tyr2026}. The same 1 GiB and 15-second
process caps applied, with five runs per instance. The executable was a
Release build with GCC 16.2 and LTO disabled. All times include startup,
parsing, and search. Tyr's raw measurements and reproducible runner are in
\path{benchmarks/results/tyr-visitall/} and
\path{benchmarks/run_tyr_visitall.py}; \texttt{just install-tyr} builds the
pinned executable under the user's local installation directory. These
measurements are a local
comparison under our stricter limits, not a reproduction of Tyr's broader
published evaluation. Tyr's greedy best-first search is also satisficing.

A* solves seven of eight instances versus six for BFS. It reduces expansions
on every instance both solve and turns \texttt{p3} from a memory failure into
a solved case. It is faster than Powerlifted in full-process time on five of
its seven solved cases (\texttt{p0,p1,p2,p5,p8}); \texttt{p8} is about
17.5 times faster. Against Tyr's full-process times, A* wins on four shallow
instances (\texttt{p0,p1,p5,p8}); \texttt{p8} is about 3.6 times faster.
Powerlifted and Tyr solve all eight and beat A* on the deeper
\texttt{p3,p4}; Tyr also beats A* on \texttt{p2}. Powerlifted's search CPU
time is only about 0.0025 seconds on \texttt{p8}; the full-process result
includes its Python driver and translation. Thus the small-case advantage
concerns deployment latency as well as search, and does not establish a
faster search engine.

\section{Limits and conclusion}
This is one PDDL family, eight selected instances, one host, and one
configuration per competitor. \texttt{astar(blind())} isolates a grounded
Fast Downward baseline rather than its strongest heuristic portfolio.
The Tyr runs used a later idle session, so small timing differences between
sessions should not be overinterpreted. Full-process timings include each
planner's startup and translation; they do not isolate search speed. The old
and new \texttt{max-ground-actions} settings bound different quantities.

Lifted joins let \texttt{folplan} reach instances that complete grounding
cannot handle within these limits. The goal-cover bound makes A* much more
effective than lifted BFS and gives lower end-to-end latency than both
comparators on four shallow cases. Powerlifted and Tyr retain full coverage
and substantially better times on the deeper instances. Generalizing the
bound beyond positive conjunctive goals and reducing search-state memory
are the clearest next steps.
